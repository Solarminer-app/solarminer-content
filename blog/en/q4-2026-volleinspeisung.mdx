Anyone running or planning a PV system faces a new calculus for surplus solar power once Germany's EEG 2027 enters into force. The government draft, approved by the federal cabinet on 29 July 2026, abolishes the fixed feed-in tariff for new installations, drops the full-feed-in bonus, and replaces the familiar 60% cap with a permanent limitation of feed-in power to 50% of installed capacity. For operators who currently sell their surplus — or who are weighing alternatives such as mining — the comparison numbers shift considerably.

One caveat up front: as of October 2026 the EEG 2027 has not been passed. The Bundestag held its first reading of the draft on 24 September 2026, and the economics committee's hearing took place on 5 October 2026. The Bundesrat has, among other things, proposed deleting the 50% cap. Everything below refers to the current draft and may still change. Existing installations keep grandfathered protection.

## What the EEG 2027 draft actually proposes

The core of the reform is the end of the 20-year guaranteed feed-in tariff for new installations from 1 January 2027. In its place comes a tiered system:

- **Transition payment:** Installations commissioned in 2027 with less than 50 kW of installed capacity receive a payment of a nominal 5.2 ct/kWh for up to 36 months (1 ct/kWh is deducted from the uniform reference value of 6.2 ct/kWh). Access then narrows step by step: in 2028 only for systems under 25 kW, from 2029 only for systems under 7 kW.
- **Direct marketing as the default:** After the transition phase, electricity is to be sold on the exchange, with a temporarily tiered direct-marketing premium.
- **End of the full-feed-in bonus:** The existing surcharge for systems that feed in everything is dropped. A single, size-independent rate applies.

In practice: registering by 31 December 2026 locks in the current rates for 20 years — since 1 August 2026 that is 7.70 ct/kWh for partial feed-in and 12.22 ct/kWh for full feed-in on systems up to 10 kWp. Anyone installing from 2027 gets a guaranteed 5.2 ct/kWh for at most three years, and exchange prices after that.

## From 60% to 50%: the new cap on feed-in power

Since the "Solarspitzengesetz" (in force since 25 February 2025), PV systems between 2 and 100 kWp without a smart meter may feed in at most 60% of their installed capacity. With a smart metering system and control unit, that limit falls away — full feed-in is then possible, but is unpaid during hours with negative exchange prices.

The EEG 2027 draft reverses the logic: new second-segment systems under 100 kW are to have their active power feed-in at the grid connection point **permanently capped at 50%** of installed capacity — regardless of smart-meter status. Full feed-in in today's sense would effectively no longer be possible; the non-feedable remainder must be self-consumed, stored, or the inverter curtailed. At the same time, the draft lowers barriers for so-called zero-feed-in systems that keep their surplus entirely — storing or curtailing instead of feeding in.

For the surplus comparison this is the decisive point: the cap applies to feeding into the grid. It does not apply to self-consumption. A miner running in your house is legally self-consumption — the 50% limit does not touch it directly, but it does change the alternative ("feed in") that mining competes against.

## Negative prices: why feed-in revenue is under pressure

Independent of the cap, the value of fed-in electricity erodes around midday. The number of hours with negative day-ahead prices in Germany has climbed for years: 301 hours (2023), 457 (2024), 573 (2025) — about 6.5% of all annual hours. The first half of 2026 already counted 299 such hours; full-year forecasts range from 700 to 900. On 1 May 2026 the day-ahead price fell to −499.99 €/MWh, after which EPEX Spot lowered the price floor to −600 €/MWh. The average negative price in 2026 so far is around −28.65 €/MWh.

For new installations commissioned since 25 February 2025 the rule is: if the spot price drops below zero in a quarter-hour, the payment is cut to zero from the very first quarter-hour (§ 51 EEG). The missed periods are appended to the funding period under § 51a EEG — but for solar systems only at half weight, and with up to 20 years of delay. Expert opinions on the EEG reform assume that 15–20% of annual hours, in some cases up to 30%, will carry negative exchange prices over the next five years.

Applied to a full-feed-in system: if 600 hours of negative prices occur in a year, roughly 9% of the annual feed-in revenue is initially lost, depending on the model. That is exactly the gap eating into the apparently safe feed-in business — and it hits full-feed-in operators disproportionately, because their entire revenue depends on the tariff.

## What this means for the feed-in vs. mining comparison

The shift is visible in the numbers:

- **Until 31.12.2026 (register in time):** 12.22 ct/kWh full feed-in or 7.70 ct/kWh partial feed-in, guaranteed for 20 years — a plannable benchmark every alternative must beat.
- **From 2027 (draft):** 5.2 ct/kWh for at most 36 months, then direct marketing at exchange prices — with the risk of negative-price hours where revenue is zero. The market value of midday solar power sits near zero in many hours.

On the mining side, SolarMiner's live data (retrieved 8 October 2026) shows: Bitcoin price €73,675, network difficulty 1.327 × 10¹⁴, current block reward 3.14 BTC. An Antminer S19k Pro runs at roughly 23.9 J/TH. That yields a mining revenue per kWh directly comparable to whichever feed-in tariff applies — plug your own system values into the [revenue calculator](/en/pv-mining-calculator). The underlying comparison is laid out in detail in [solar surplus and mining 2027](/de/pv-ueberschuss-mining-2027).

The structural difference: feeding in delivers a guaranteed or exchange-dependent payout per kWh with no extra hardware. Mining converts the same kWh into revenue that depends on the Bitcoin price, difficulty, and hardware efficiency — but the miner counts as self-consumption and is untouched by the 50% cap. As the feed-in tariff falls to 5.2 ct/kWh and to zero in negative-price hours, the bar that mining has to clear comes down.

## Where mining is still not the better choice in 2027

The honest side of the ledger:

- **Registering in 2026 is a strong deal.** 12.22 ct/kWh for full feed-in, guaranteed for 20 years, is a bond-like yield with no hardware risk, no difficulty risk, and no price risk. Mining has to beat that — and often loses once purchase price, maintenance, and residual value are included.
- **Existing installations are unaffected.** If you already feed in under a tariff, nothing changes for you. The feed-in vs. mining question only arises for *additional* surplus.
- **Noise has a price.** An ASIC drawing around 3 kW continuously is unbearable in living space without serious acoustic decoupling. Quiet installation eats budget and space.
- **A battery is often the sturdier answer.** If the goal is backup power, higher self-consumption, or protection from negative-price hours, storage delivers those directly — mining does not.
- **Mining carries market risk.** The Bitcoin price can fall and difficulty rises structurally. Comparing a 20-year guaranteed tariff to a revenue-variable business means comparing different risk classes.

In short: the EEG 2027 makes feeding in less attractive for new systems and improves the relative position of surplus mining — but mining is no guarantee because of it, and for grandfathered systems, feeding in usually remains the sounder base.
